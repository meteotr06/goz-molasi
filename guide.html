Eye health at a screen: does the 20-20-20 rule actually work?

Short answer: the 20-20-20 rule is an expert recommendation, not a proven treatment. Those particular numbers were chosen in the 1990s because they are easy to remember. What is well documented is the problem itself, and the fact that taking regular breaks reduces symptoms. This guide separates what is solid from what is thin, with sources for both.

What is digital eye strain?

Digital eye strain — also called computer vision syndrome — is the collection of symptoms that show up after long stretches at a screen: tired eyes, dryness or watering, burning, blurred vision, and headaches.

It is common. A review pooling 45 studies found that roughly 66% of screen workers report at least one symptom. The three most frequent were blurred vision (34%), tired eyes (32%) and watering (31%).

Two things are worth saying plainly. First, the symptoms are real and measurable. Second, they are reversible — there is no evidence that screen use causes lasting damage to the eye.

Source: Scientific Reports, 2023 — systematic review and meta-analysis of 45 studies

Why it happens: three separate mechanisms

1. Your blink rate collapses

You normally blink about 15 times a minute. In a classic study of 104 office workers, blink rate fell to 10 per minute while reading a book and 7 per minute while looking at a screen.

But the count is only half the story. At a screen, most blinks are incomplete — the lids never fully meet. A partial blink does not spread the tear film properly across the surface.

Source: Tsubota & Nakamori, New England Journal of Medicine, 1993 · Patel et al., Optometry and Vision Science, 1991

2. The focusing muscle stays contracted

To focus up close, the ciliary muscle inside the eye contracts. Hold that for hours and it simply stays contracted. Looking into the distance is what releases it — which is the entire logic behind the rule.

3. Posture and distance

Sitting still at a screen loads the neck, shoulders and lower back too. Part of what people call "eye strain" is really the whole body being held in one position.

Where the 20-20-20 rule comes from

The rule says: every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet away (about 6 metres) for 20 seconds.

It is generally credited to the Californian optometrist Jeffrey Anshel, who proposed it in the 1990s. The numbers were not derived from a trial. They were chosen because three twenties are easy to remember — and Anshel has said as much. That does not make the advice wrong; it means the precise figures should not be treated as findings.

Why 6 metres specifically?

In eye care, 6 metres counts as optical infinity — it is only 0.17 dioptres away from true infinity. Look that far and the focusing muscle relaxes essentially completely. Looking further brings no additional benefit, which is why "the far wall of the room" is usually enough.

Does it work? The state of the evidence

This is where most articles get vague. Here is the honest breakdown.

Weak evidence The numbers themselves

A 2023 study tested the rule directly and found that 20-second breaks every 20 minutes were not sufficient to relax the focusing system; breaks every 10 minutes performed better. The authors' conclusion was blunt: the specific numbers are not justified by evidence.

Source: Johnson & Rosenfield, 20-20-20 Rule: Are These Numbers Justified? Optometry and Vision Science, 2023;100(1):52-56

Moderate evidence The reminder itself

What holds up better is the habit rather than the arithmetic. In a 2023 controlled study, two weeks of using a break reminder reduced subjective symptoms compared with no reminder. A survey of 795 university students likewise found fewer symptoms among those who periodically looked into the distance.

In other words: the value is in being interrupted at all, not in the number 20.

Source: Talens-Estarelles et al., Contact Lens and Anterior Eye, 2023;46(2):101744

Very low evidence Musculoskeletal benefit

A 2025 Cochrane review of work-break schedules for preventing musculoskeletal symptoms rated the certainty of evidence as very low. Breaks probably help, but the research is not strong enough to say by how much.

Source: Luger et al., Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2025

Common myths

"Screens permanently damage your eyes"

No. There is no evidence of lasting structural damage from screen use. The symptoms are genuine but they resolve with rest. What screens can do indirectly is displace time spent outdoors, which matters for children — see below.

"20-20-20 prevents myopia"

It does not, on current evidence. Myopia in children is driven far more by how much daylight they get than by break schedules. Distance breaks are a reasonable habit, but do not expect them to change refraction.

"Blue-light filters fix eye strain"

This one is contradicted by the strongest evidence we have. In 2023 Cochrane — the most rigorous body in evidence appraisal — pooled 17 randomised controlled trials from six countries. The conclusion: blue-light filtering spectacle lenses do not reduce eye strain from computer use compared with ordinary lenses.

The same review found no randomised evidence at all that these lenses protect the retina. The good news is that no harm was found either — reported side effects were mild and temporary.

The reason is straightforward: the symptoms come mostly from reduced blinking and a contracted focusing muscle. Neither has anything to do with the colour of the light. You still blink five times a minute while wearing a filter.

Blue light's effect on sleep is a separate question and is better documented — but there the answer is to use screens less in the evening, not to buy glasses.

Source: Singh et al., Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2023, Issue 8, CD013244 — 17 randomised controlled trials

What to do in practice

Knowing where the evidence stands, a sensible approach looks like this:

  1. Take breaks on a schedule. The benefit is in the interruption. Twenty minutes is a reasonable starting point; stretch it to 30 if it breaks your concentration, shorten it to 10 if your eyes tire quickly.
  2. Actually look far away. Six metres is enough — out of a window, or the far corner of the room. Looking at your phone is not a break.
  3. Blink deliberately. Ten full blinks during the break, letting the lids close completely each time.
  4. Position the screen properly. An arm's length away, slightly below eye level.
  5. Fix the environment. Dry, air-conditioned rooms make dryness worse. Keep airflow off your face.
  6. Stand up. The neck and back benefit more from this than your eyes do.

What actually helps tired eyes?

There is no single fix, but several things have measurable effects. Roughly in order of impact:

1. The break itself

Looking far relaxes the ciliary muscle; blinking deliberately renews the tear film. These two act directly on the two main causes. This is where the evidence is strongest — and again, the benefit lies in the regular reminder, not in the exact numbers.

2. Artificial tears

If your eyes sting, burn or feel dry, preservative-free artificial tears ease the symptoms. They are available without prescription. If you will use them more than four times a day, choose preservative-free — preservatives can irritate the surface with frequent use.

3. Humidity and airflow

Air conditioning and central heating dry the air; a draught aimed at your face evaporates the tear film quickly. Turning the fan away and putting a bowl of water in the room in winter are simple things that work.

4. Screen position

The top edge of the screen should be below eye level. Looking upward opens the eyelid wider, so more surface is exposed and tears evaporate faster. Distance: an arm's length, roughly 50–70 cm.

5. Brightness balance

The screen should not be noticeably brighter than the wall behind it. Working at full brightness in a dark room strains the eyes. A simple test: if the rest of the room looks like a dark cave when you look at the screen, turn the brightness down.

What does not help

Blue-light filtering glasses, as explained above. There is also no evidence that "eye vitamin" supplements help with screen fatigue.

Why do eyes get dry?

At a screen your blink rate drops from about 15 a minute to 5–7. But the real problem is that most of those blinks are incomplete — the lids do not fully meet. A partial blink does not spread the tear film evenly.

The tear film has three layers: oil on the outside, water in the middle, mucin underneath. The oil layer slows evaporation, and it comes from glands along the edge of the eyelid (the meibomian glands). What empties those glands is a complete blink. Blink halfway and no oil arrives, the watery layer evaporates quickly, and the eye dries out.

That is why "blink fully ten times" during a break is not empty advice: the point of the lids meeting is to squeeze those glands.

If you wear contact lenses, dryness may be more pronounced, because the lens splits the tear film in two. Breaks matter more in this group.

Source: Craig et al., TFOS DEWS II Definition and Classification Report, The Ocular Surface, 2017;15(3):276-283

Children, screen time and myopia

This is what parents ask about most, and the answer is not the one most people expect: the decisive factor is not screen time, it is time spent outdoors.

A 2024 meta-analysis pooled data from 12,922 children aged 6–16. The result is a clear dose-response relationship. Compared with a child who spends 3.5 hours a week outdoors:

  • 7 hours a week outdoors → risk of developing myopia 20% lower
  • 16.3 hours → 53% lower
  • 27 hours → 69% lower

Put another way: roughly 76 extra minutes outdoors per day halves the risk of myopia onset.

One important detail: this protection works in children who are not yet myopic. In children who have already developed myopia, time outdoors does not appear to slow progression. It is a preventive measure, not a treatment.

The mechanism is thought to involve the sheer intensity of daylight. Indoors you get 300–500 lux; outdoors, even on an overcast day, it is above 10,000 lux. So "go outside" here means being outside — not exercising.

As for screen time itself: screens do not damage the eye. But hours at a screen are hours not spent outdoors, and that indirect effect is the real one. The better question is not "how many hours of screen" but "how many hours outside".

Source: Kido et al., Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2024 · Ophthalmic Research, 2024 — meta-analysis of 12,922 participants

Frequently asked questions

Isn't 20 seconds too short?

It may well be. A 2023 study suggested 20 seconds is not enough to relax the focusing muscle and that 1–2 minutes would be more appropriate. Still, 20 seconds beats no break at all, and it is far more likely to actually happen.

Does closing my eyes count?

Yes. A closed eye is also resting, and it helps the tear film renew. The extra benefit of looking far away is relaxing the focusing muscle; doing both is best.

I wear glasses or contact lenses — does that change things?

Dryness can be more pronounced with contact lenses, because the lens splits the tear film. Breaks matter more in this group.

How many breaks before I notice a difference?

In the 2023 study, subjective symptoms decreased after two weeks of using a reminder. There is no fixed timeline; consistency is what matters.
